Every person who contacts Curia — whether through email, Signal, or the HTTP API — is a contact with a **capability tier**. Before Curia takes any action on a sender's behalf, it checks who they are, how confident it is in their identity, and whether the channel they're using is trusted enough for what they're asking. These checks are deterministic: a sender either has permission or they don't.

<Warning>
  New contacts start at the **`unknown`** tier. An unknown sender isn't ignored — Curia will read and reply to them under read-only constraints (it won't take actions, share your private context, or commit anything on your behalf). Contacts move up to **`known`** automatically as a real relationship becomes evident, and you can promote anyone to **`trusted`** explicitly. See [capability tiers](#capability-tiers) below.
</Warning>

## How trust scores work

Every inbound message from an external sender carries a `messageTrustScore` between 0.0 and 1.0. Curia computes this score in the dispatch layer before the coordinator ever sees the message.

The score combines three inputs:

```
messageTrustScore =
  (channel trust weight × 0.4)
  + (contact confidence × 0.4)
  − (content risk penalty, up to 0.2)
```

**Channel trust** reflects what the authentication protocol can guarantee about sender identity:

| Channel | Trust level | Normalized weight |
|---|---|---|
| CLI | High | 1.0 |
| Signal | High | 1.0 |
| HTTP API | Medium | 0.6 |
| Email | Low | 0.3 |

Separately, a contact's **capability tier** indicates how much Curia trusts a specific person, regardless of channel. See [capability tiers](#capability-tiers) below for the full model — `principal` (you) outranks all others and bypasses PII redaction, outbound content filters, and trust-score thresholds.

**Contact confidence** accumulates over time as signal builds. A brand-new email sender starts at zero. A CEO-verified Signal contact with a long interaction history may carry 0.95.

Contact confidence is computed automatically by the **confidence scoring pipeline** — it updates on every qualifying event rather than being set manually:

| Event | Effect |
|---|---|
| Inbound message received | Incremental increase based on message history |
| Outbound reply sent | Incremental increase (bidirectional contact) |
| CEO explicit trust grant | Larger positive boost |
| Verified identity pairing confirmed | Positive boost |

The pipeline supports two modes: **incremental** (triggered on each qualifying event, applied as a delta) and **full recompute** (recalculates from all stored history). Both modes converge to the same value. You never set `contact_confidence` directly — it is always derived from observable history.

**Content risk** reduces the score when the dispatch layer detects injection-like patterns in the message body. Clean messages incur no penalty.

## Capability tiers

A contact's **tier** is the single ordered gate on what Curia will do for them. It replaced the older split of a `status` field (`provisional`/`confirmed`/`blocked`) and a separate `trust_level`. From lowest to highest:

| Tier | Who gets it | What they can drive |
|---|---|---|
| `blocked` | Senders you've blocked | Nothing — messages may be dropped |
| `unknown` | New / unrecognized senders (the default) | Read-only: Curia may reply or ask a question, but takes no actions and shares no private context |
| `known` | Recognized correspondents (reached automatically) | Routine actions and light context |
| `trusted` | People you've explicitly elevated — EA, CFO, board | Elevated actions and disclosure |
| `principal` | You, the CEO | Full authority |

Alongside the tier, each contact has a **kind** describing what it is: `person`, `organization` (a company, not an individual), `automated` (a noreply/newsletter sender — kept out of your way), `agent` (one of Curia's own agents), or `principal` (you). Kind is a label, not a permission gate; `automated` senders are the one kind that's actively filtered out of your attention.

The `principal` tier outranks all others and is set structurally at startup — it can't be assigned with `contact-set-tier`, and a merge can never delete or downgrade it. Tier comparisons use a `meetsMinimumTier()` ordinal check, so `principal` satisfies any gate that requires `trusted`, `known`, etc.

You set a tier conversationally — "treat Dana as trusted" runs the `contact-set-tier` tool (it refuses to mint a `principal`). Promotions to `known` happen on their own; see [automatic elevation](#automatic-elevation) below.

### Score examples

| Sender | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Brand-new email sender | ~0.12 | Low channel trust (0.3 × 0.4) + zero contact confidence |
| CEO-verified Signal contact | ~0.80 | High channel trust (1.0 × 0.4) + high confidence (0.95 × 0.4) |
| Unknown Signal sender | ~0.40 | High channel trust + zero contact confidence |
| Known email contact (long history) | ~0.52 | Low channel trust + high confidence |

## Action thresholds

The coordinator checks the sender's `messageTrustScore` before taking action. If the score falls below the threshold for the requested action, the request is declined.

| Action category | Minimum score | What this means |
|---|---|---|
| Information queries | 0.2 | Any authenticated message qualifies |
| Scheduling | 0.5 | Medium trust channel or a known email contact |
| Data export | 0.8 | High-trust channel and a verified contact |
| Financial actions | 0.8 | High-trust channel and a verified contact |

These thresholds are configured in `config/default.yaml` under `security.trust_thresholds` and can be adjusted without a code change. Startup fails if this block is missing or malformed.

<Note>
  Trust thresholds do not apply to you (the CEO). Messages arriving via CLI, or from the `principal` contact, are always trusted regardless of score. Curia never explains the trust system or mentions scores to external senders.
</Note>

## Email sender verification

Email is the highest-risk channel because From headers can be spoofed. Curia adds an extra layer on top of the trust score: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC validation.

Your email provider performs these checks at the server level. The email channel adapter reads the `Authentication-Results` headers on every inbound message and maps them to a `senderVerified` flag:

- `senderVerified: true` — SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all pass
- `senderVerified: false` — any check fails, or headers are absent (fails closed)

When a message arrives with `senderVerified: false`, the coordinator will not take consequential actions — financial, data, or access changes — without confirmation via a verified channel like Signal or CLI.

## Handling unknown senders

When Curia receives a message from someone not in your contacts, it doesn't queue it for you to triage. Each channel has an **unknown-sender policy** — either `allow` (the default for email and Signal) or `ignore`.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Message arrives from an unknown sender">
    The dispatch layer looks up the sender's channel identifier (`email address`, `Signal number`) in the contacts database. No match is found, so Curia auto-creates a contact at the **`unknown`** tier and writes an audit event with the sender identifier, channel, and routing decision.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Curia handles it in low-trust mode">
    Under the `allow` policy the message goes straight to the coordinator with read-only constraints: Curia may reply or ask a clarifying question, but it won't take actions, share your private context, or reveal that it's operating under restrictions. There's no holding step — the sender gets a timely, careful response. Under the `ignore` policy the message is silently dropped.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Automated senders are filtered">
    Before replying, Curia classifies the sender. Noreply addresses, newsletters, and bulk senders are marked `kind=automated` and kept out of your way — they don't generate the attention an actual person would.
  </Step>
  <Step title="You identify or elevate them">
    Once you tell Curia who someone is (or it works it out from your correspondence), the channel identity links to a real contact and the tier rises to `known`. You can elevate further at any time: "treat Dana as trusted." To shut someone out, block them — their messages are dropped without acknowledgment.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Automatic contact creation from email

When Curia processes an inbound email, it auto-creates `unknown`-tier contacts for every participant in the From, To, and CC headers that isn't already in your contacts. This builds your contact database naturally as emails flow through.

To protect against spam campaigns that flood your contacts with thousands of junk entries, auto-creation is rate-limited:

| Limit | Default | What happens when hit |
|---|---|---|
| **Per message** | 10 | Remaining participants in that email are skipped |
| **Per hour** | 100 per email account | All further auto-creation for that account pauses until the window resets |

When a limit is hit, Curia notifies you via email (at most once per limit type per hour). Skipped participants are not lost — they will be auto-created if they email you directly in the future.

Both limits are configurable in `config/local.yaml`:

```yaml
contact_creation_limits:
  max_per_message: 10
  max_per_hour: 100
```

<Note>
Existing contacts are not counted toward the limits — only genuinely new contact creations. If a 20-person thread has 18 known contacts, only the 2 new ones count.
</Note>

## Automatic elevation

An `unknown`-tier contact is elevated to **`known`** automatically when Curia sees evidence of a real relationship — no confirmation step from you, and no fixed daily sweep. Three kinds of signal drive it:

1. **Correspondence** — when Curia (or you) sends an outbound message to the contact, the outbound gateway raises their tier to at least `known` before the message goes out. Two-way communication is enough.
2. **Judgment** — a weekly background scan and the dispatch-layer scoring pipeline weigh signals like email domain, relationship context, and communication patterns, elevating contacts that cross the confidence threshold.
3. **Backfill** — a one-shot maintenance script re-evaluates existing `unknown` contacts against the same criteria.

<Note>
  Automatic elevation only ever reaches `known`. Moving a contact to `trusted` or `principal` always requires an explicit step from you. Elevation also never marks a *self-claimed* channel identity as verified — that still requires your confirmation.
</Note>

## Adding contacts proactively

You don't have to wait for someone to reach out. Tell Curia about people directly in conversation:

> "Add Sarah Chen, CFO at Acme. Her email is sarah.chen@acme.com and she's on Signal at +15550001111."

Curia creates a contact record, links both channel identities as verified, and assigns the CFO role with its default permissions. You can also update existing contacts:

> "Jenna's new work email is jenna.torres@acme.com."

> "Grant Jenna permission to send emails on my behalf."

All contact mutations are confirmed with you before they're written, and every change is audit-logged.

## Contact profile attributes

Beyond identity and trust, Curia keeps a small set of **canonical profile attributes** directly on each contact record: preferred name, job title, organization, primary email and phone, timezone, locale, location, pronouns, LinkedIn URL, a short bio, and birthday. These are stored as structured fields on the contact itself — not as free-floating knowledge-graph facts — so agents read a reliable value instead of inferring one from a list of remembered notes. That distinction matters: it's what stops Curia from guessing a stale address or timezone when composing on your behalf.

You set them the same conversational way as anything else ("Sarah's title is now VP of Sales", "her timezone is US Pacific"), and Curia writes them via the `contact-update` tool (phone numbers are normalized to E.164). Richer, narrative knowledge about a person — relationships, history, preferences — still lives in the knowledge graph; the canonical attributes are just the handful of stable fields worth pinning down. The console's contact view exposes the same fields for direct editing.

## Contact deduplication

Curia runs a weekly background scan for likely duplicate contacts — for example, "Jenna Torres" and "J. Torres" who share the same email address. When potential duplicates are detected, Curia presents them to you for review:

> "I noticed two contacts that look like the same person:
> - Jenna Torres (CFO, verified email jenna@acme.com)
> - J. Torres (no role, email jenna@acme.com)
>
> I'd merge them into Jenna Torres (CFO). Want me to proceed?"

Curia never auto-merges contacts. You confirm every merge.

## How authorization works

Authorization runs through a layered check on every request from a non-principal sender:

1. **Tier gate** — `unknown` and `blocked` contacts get zero permissions before anything else is considered. The contact must be at least `known` to hold any permission at all.
2. **Per-contact overrides** — explicit grants and denials you've set for this specific contact. These always win over role defaults.
3. **Role defaults** — the default permissions and denials for the contact's role (CFO, board member, direct report, advisor, etc.).
4. **Channel trust gate** — even if role and overrides permit an action, the channel must be trusted enough. Financial and data actions require a high-trust channel.

Separately, a **consequential-action gate** weighs the *initiating* contact's tier against the action's stakes: a `known` contact can get a reply to themselves, but a third-party-facing or higher-stakes action escalates to you for a decision. When the call isn't clear-cut, an escalation judge classifies it and, by design, escalates rather than guesses.

The outcome is one of: `Allowed`, `Denied`, `Blocked by channel trust`, or `Needs CEO decision`.

```
Verified contact requests an action
         │
         ▼
1. Check per-contact overrides
   → Explicit grant? → Allow
   → Explicit denial? → Deny
   → No override? → continue
         │
         ▼
2. Check role defaults
   → In default_permissions? → Allow
   → In default_deny? → Deny
   → Not listed? → Escalate to CEO
         │
         ▼
3. Check channel trust gate
   → Channel trust meets action requirement? → Allow
   → Channel trust too low? → Escalate to CEO
```

<Warning>
  Authorization is deterministic. The coordinator cannot override the permission system, even if a request seems reasonable. If the system says "Denied," the action is denied — Curia declines politely and, if appropriate, lets the sender know they can reach out through a more secure channel.
</Warning>

### Trust also gates what Curia learns

The same trust model governs writes to the [knowledge graph](/core-concepts/memory), not just outbound actions. When an inbound message would trigger a memory write — storing a fact, extracting facts or relationships from the content — that write is blocked if the initiating sender is at the `unknown` or `blocked` tier. This keeps an untrusted party from seeding the knowledge graph with self-serving or poisoned "facts" through crafted message content. Curia still reads and responds within the sender's tier; it just won't persist new knowledge learned from an untrusted source.

## Channel identity status

Every channel identity (email address, phone number, etc.) linked to a contact carries a `status` field, separate from whether it has been verified:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `active` | The identity is current and preferred for outbound use |
| `defunct` | The identity is no longer valid (e.g., old email address) |
| `bounced` | Outbound delivery to this address has failed |

Curia prefers `active` identities when assembling outbound context. When non-active identities would be used — for example, if a contact's only linked email is `defunct` — the Contact Specialist warns the Coordinator before suggesting them. You can update an identity's status directly:

> "Mark sarah's old acme.com email as defunct — she's at beacon.com now."

Curia uses the `contact-set-identity-status` tool. Status is orthogonal to verification: a `verified` identity can be `defunct`, and an `active` identity may be `self_claimed` until you confirm it.

## Self-claimed identities

If someone messages on a new channel and claims to be an existing contact ("Hi, it's Jenna"), that identity is tagged `self_claimed` and treated as unverified until you confirm it. Curia never auto-promotes self-claimed identities to verified status. An unverified identity is gated the same as an unknown sender for any consequential action.

## Tiers at a glance

| Tier | Who | What they can drive |
|---|---|---|
| **`blocked`** | Contacts you've blocked | None. Messages are dropped without response. |
| **`unknown`** | New / unrecognized senders (the default) | None — Curia replies under read-only constraints but takes no action. |
| **`known`** | Recognized correspondents (reached automatically) | Role defaults + any per-contact overrides; routine actions |
| **`trusted`** | People you've explicitly elevated | Elevated actions and disclosure |
| **`principal`** | You, the CEO | Full authority |
