Curia's autonomy engine gives you a single dial to control how independently your AI team operates. A score from 0 to 100 determines whether Curia acts on its own, asks before acting, drafts without sending, or sticks to pure advice. The score applies across all agents, channels, and tools — one setting, consistent behavior everywhere. You can read it or change it at any time with a plain-English instruction.

## The five autonomy bands

The score maps to one of five named bands. Each band has a behavioral description that is injected into the Coordinator's system prompt on every task, so Curia self-governs in real time — no restart required.

The band, not the tier of the contact Curia is dealing with, decides when Curia asks you first.

| Band | Score range | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Full** | 90–100 | Acts for you, including speaking and committing for you. Puts notable actions in the digest. Asks first when an outsider seems to be steering it, and before committing you on payments, pricing, contracts or legal positions. |
| **Spot-check** | 80–89 | Takes on work, coordinates, and speaks for you on routine matters without asking. Asks first before committing you to dates, deliverables, terms or introductions, and on anything involving money or legal positions. Reports what it did in the digest. |
| **Approval Required** | 70–79 | Takes on work and coordinates (logistics, confirmations, bringing people in) without asking. Asks you first before it speaks for you or commits you to anything. |
| **Draft Only** | 60–69 | Drafts rather than sends. You confirm new work; internal logistics within confirmed work can proceed. Drafts and asks surface in the digest. |
| **Restricted** | < 60 | Proposes and drafts only. Takes no action with an external effect. |

A direct instruction from you counts as your approval: Curia carries it out and fills in any open details itself, taking the conservative choice and saying which it made. This applies when Curia knows the instruction is really from you (see [Who counts as you](#who-counts-as-you)).

<Note>
Curia defaults to **75 (Approval Required)** on first deployment. This is a conservative starting point — Curia does what you tell it, but confirms before speaking or committing for you on its own initiative until you decide to extend more independence.
</Note>

<Note>
Before v0.45, a contact's trust tier decided which actions needed approval. That policy is still available until a later release removes it: set `autonomy.gating: tier` in `config/local.yaml` to keep it. See [Configuration](/references/configuration#gating).
</Note>

## What Curia checks before it sends

When Curia writes to someone on your behalf, an independent reviewer checks the message before it goes:

- **Is someone steering Curia?** If an outsider seems to be pushing Curia to act against your interests, the send is held for you at every band.
- **Is every claim about you backed up?** Statements about you must be supported by your own words or by something Curia looked up during that turn. A message with an unsupported claim goes back for one rewrite. If the rewrite still has the problem, it is held or not sent.
- **What does it commit you to?** Routine replies (thanks, acknowledgements, scheduling logistics) can go out on their own at 80 and above. Commitments (new dates, deliverables, terms or introductions) need a score of 90 or above. Anything about money or legal positions always asks you first.
- **Does it share personal details?** Each personal detail is checked against who authorized it. See [Personal details](#personal-details).

If the reviewer can't be reached, Curia holds autonomous sends for you rather than sending them unchecked.

## Personal details

Before a message carries someone's personal details (a phone number, an address, a Canadian SIN, a card number and similar), Curia checks who authorized each one:

- **You asked for it.** Your own instruction, on a channel where Curia knows it's you, releases the details you asked to share.
- **Someone else asked for it.** A trusted contact's request, a stranger's ask, or text quoted inside an earlier message goes to you first. Curia asks you, for example "Dana wants your mobile number to book the call. Share it?" Your answer holds for that thread.
- **Nobody authorized it.** Curia rewrites the message without the detail. A stranger Curia has no reason to deal with gets a polite decline, and you hear about it in the digest.

Canadian SINs are recognized by their checksum, so ordinary nine-digit numbers such as order IDs aren't mistaken for one.

## Who counts as you

A direct instruction counts as your approval only when Curia can tell it's really you. Messages from these count:

- the web console, in chat and in voice calls, while you're signed in;
- Signal, both messages and calls;
- Slack;
- email that passes the sender checks.

SMS, the local CLI and phone calls identified only by caller ID don't count as authenticated.

## Checking your current score

You can ask Curia about its autonomy score from any channel using plain language:

<Tip>
Ask Curia directly: **"What is your current autonomy score?"**

Curia will report its score, the current band name, what that means in practice, and the last few score changes with timestamps.
</Tip>

Example response:

```
Autonomy score: 75 — Approval Required

At this level, I'll carry out what you ask me to do, but I'll confirm
before sending email or creating commitments on my own initiative.
Research and summarization proceed independently.

Recent changes:
  2026-04-03  75 → 75  (approval-required)  "starting point"  — ceo
```

## Changing your score

<Tip>
Tell Curia: **"Set your autonomy score to 85"** from the CLI, Signal, or any channel you trust.

You can add an optional reason: "Set your autonomy score to 85 — I want you to proceed on routine emails without asking."
</Tip>

Score changes take effect immediately on the next task — no restart required. The Coordinator loads the current score from Postgres at the start of every task, so a change you make mid-session applies on the very next action.

<Warning>
Only the CEO can change the autonomy score, and only in the moment. `set-autonomy` is an **elevated** tool: it runs solely during a live CEO turn, so a scheduled job or a woken task that merely carries the CEO's lineage cannot raise (or lower) the score on its own. Requests from external email senders or unverified contacts are rejected.
</Warning>

## How to calibrate your score

A practical approach for new deployments:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Start at 75 (the default)">
    Approval Required means Curia does what you ask, and asks before sending anything or making commitments you didn't ask for. This gives you full visibility into what it would do without any risk of surprises.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Watch it work for a week">
    Pay attention to which confirmations feel unnecessary. If Curia is asking about things you always say yes to, your score is too low for your comfort level.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move to 80–85 when you're comfortable">
    Spot-check lets Curia handle routine replies and logistics on its own and report them in the digest. It still asks before committing you to anything. You stay informed without being interrupted.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Reserve 90+ for high-trust workflows">
    Full autonomy lets Curia make commitments for you, such as agreeing to a date or a deliverable. It's appropriate once you've seen Curia handle that kind of work reliably. Money and legal matters still come to you first.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Hard execution gates

The autonomy score isn't only guidance injected into prompts — it's enforced as a hard gate at runtime. When Curia attempts to invoke a tool, the execution layer checks the live score against the tool's declared `action_risk` floor. If the score is too low, the tool is blocked before it runs.

This means the bands are binding. At **Draft Only** (60–69), outbound sends are blocked regardless of what the coordinator decides. At **Restricted** (< 60), all non-read tools are blocked. The coordinator cannot reason its way around the gate.

## What happens when Curia can't act

A blocked or held action doesn't fail silently. Curia creates a numbered **approval request**: a record of what it was trying to do and why it stopped. It sends you the request once, on the channel your standing preferences name, so you can allow that one action without changing your global autonomy score.

<Tip>
Reply **"approve 3"** or **"deny 3"**, using the number from the request. On Signal and Slack you can also react 👍 or 👎 to the request message. Ask **"What's waiting for my approval?"** to see everything still open.
</Tip>

Nothing needing your attention lingers unseen: approval requests notify you directly, and any task that has stalled, hit a limit, or is blocked on you escalates to you with the reason (see [Tasks and the backlog](/core-concepts/scheduling#tasks-and-the-backlog)). You can also ask **"what's waiting on me?"** any time, or fold these items into an [on-demand morning briefing](/examples/daily-morning-briefing).

Curia also brings them up on its own. Whenever you message it while approvals are pending, stored facts need re-confirming, or tasks are waiting on you, it handles what you asked first, then mentions the oldest waiting item in a sentence — once per conversation, not on every turn. See [Approval workflow](/security/approval-workflow) for the full picture.

## Spot checks in the digest

Most of what Curia does at the higher bands, you never see as it happens. To keep you in the loop, your digest includes a few samples of work Curia did on its own:

```
Spot check (optional, reply with a number):
1. <summary>
2. <summary>
```

Reply with a number and a verdict, for example **"2 bad, that was a renewal notice"**. Your verdicts are the strongest evidence the [automatic score adjustment](/security/autonomy-engine#automatic-score-adjustment) uses. Answering is optional, and silence records nothing.

## Autonomy web UI

**Settings → Behaviour → Autonomy** in the console shows the current score and band, a slider to change it with an optional reason, and the recent changes, including automatic adjustments made by the system.
